Translational Research

Translating methodological research into practical insights for applied social and organizational research.

About This Page

A collection of translational research notes focused on bridging the gap between methodological research and applied practice.

My goal is to make research methods—particularly in psychometrics and quantitative modeling—more accessible and actionable for real-world applications. There is often a disconnect between methodological advances and how research is actually conducted in practice. At the same time, there is a gap between psychometric theory and its use in applied social and organizational research.

These notes are an attempt to close those gaps by translating technical ideas into practical insights for researchers and practitioners.

While I occasionally draw on examples from published research, the goal is not to critique individual studies or authors. Instead, I aim to highlight common methodological patterns and practices that are widely used, but not always well aligned with underlying statistical principles.

  • Why underpowered mediation studies remain a problem
    March 2026 · Power analysis · Mediation · Organizational research

    A translational note on why sample size assumptions from simple models often fail in mediation research, and how this affects the reliability of empirical findings.

    I approach business and social science research as a form of science that should be held to rigorous standards. Throughout my PhD training, I have often observed that statistical methods are learned and applied in a “procedural” way—where researchers replicate existing practices without fully understanding the underlying assumptions and mathematical foundations. This can lead to the uncritical use of methods that are not always appropriate for the research question.

    This issue may partially contribute to broader challenges such as the replication crisis in psychology and related fields. In many applied domains, errors in design or analysis have tangible consequences—for example, in engineering, flawed calculations can lead to structural failure and clear accountability. In contrast, in academic research, the consequences of methodological shortcomings (e.g., insufficient reporting of model fit, lack of power analysis) are often less immediate or visible.

    As a result, the current incentive structure may not always encourage deep engagement with psychometric and statistical principles. These notes are motivated, in part, by a desire to promote more careful, transparent, and principled use of quantitative methods in applied research.
