Recently, I attended a seminar on applied research methods. One of the speakers discussed a brief report from *JAMA Psychiatry* titled *“Interpretation Issues With the Patient Health Questionnaire Instructions.”*  

In many clinical settings, patients are asked to complete the PHQ-9 as an initial screening tool for depression. The study raises an important question: do responses to the PHQ-9 reflect the **frequency** of symptoms, or the **degree to which symptoms are bothersome**? The authors argue that the measure may be widely misinterpreted, raising concerns about its validity in both research and clinical decision-making.

I agree that more validation work is needed in clinical and medical research. However, I also have several questions regarding the conclusions of this study.

---

### 1. Measurement invariance

Before comparing responses between the general population and a clinical sample, should measurement invariance be established first?

In other words, do these groups interpret the items in the same way? Without establishing invariance, differences between groups may reflect differences in interpretation rather than true differences in depression severity.

---

### 2. Sample size and power

The general population sample (N = 503) is larger than the clinical sample (N = 349). Is this typical in medical research?

More importantly, the paper does not report a power analysis. Without this information, it is unclear whether the study is adequately powered to detect meaningful differences. Unequal sample sizes can also reduce statistical power and complicate group comparisons.

---

### 3. Gender imbalance

Among the 349 clinical participants, only 120 are male (216 are female). This raises additional questions:

- Do male and female participants interpret the items differently?  
- Should measurement invariance be tested across gender as well?  
- Why is there a higher proportion of females diagnosed with depression in this sample?

When working with imbalanced samples, it is important to examine whether results are robust across subgroups.

---

### On the response to the critique

Given that *JAMA Psychiatry* is a leading journal, the original developers of the scale responded to these concerns. One argument they made is that the PHQ-9 has been widely used, cited in over 30,000 publications, and translated into many languages.

While widespread use demonstrates utility, it does not necessarily guarantee validity.

A measure can be widely adopted and still be used inconsistently or interpreted differently across contexts. From a measurement perspective, accumulated usage should not replace careful validation.

---

### A broader question: what are we measuring?

This discussion also raises a more fundamental issue: why do we measure depression primarily in terms of frequency?

What about **intensity**?

Depression is likely a multidimensional construct. Focusing only on frequency may miss important aspects of the experience. While there may be practical reasons for using frequency-based measures (e.g., simplicity, ease of scoring), it is worth questioning whether this approach fully captures the construct of interest.

---

### Takeaway

This is not an argument against the PHQ-9 specifically. Rather, it highlights a broader issue in applied measurement:

- Scales are often treated as universal tools  
- Assumptions about interpretation are rarely tested  
- Validation is often context-specific, not general  

From a psychometric perspective, we should be cautious about assuming that a single scale functions equivalently across populations, contexts, and purposes.
