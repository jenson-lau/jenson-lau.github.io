Why underpowered mediation studies remain a problem

March 2026

Many mediation studies in organizational behavior (e.g., Academy of Management Journal, Journal of Applied Psychology, and Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes) rely on overly optimistic sample size assumptions. A key issue is that researchers often extend power analysis results from simple regression models (e.g., t-tests or ANOVA via G*Power) without verifying whether these assumptions hold in mediation settings.

In principle, each hypothesis should be supported by its own power analysis. However, in organizational research—where studies frequently follow a strong hypothesis framework (e.g., H1: X → Y; H2: mediation via M; H3: moderated mediation by W)—it is common practice to conduct a single power analysis and assume that the resulting sample size is sufficient for all hypotheses. This assumption is rarely justified.

In my work, I found that approximately 50% of mediation studies in organizational science are underpowered. This has important implications for how we interpret empirical findings, particularly with respect to false negatives and unstable effect estimates.

Power analysis should therefore be treated as a design problem rather than a procedural step. Model structure, the inclusion of covariates, and the type of variables (e.g., continuous vs. binary) can all materially influence statistical power and should be explicitly considered in study design.
